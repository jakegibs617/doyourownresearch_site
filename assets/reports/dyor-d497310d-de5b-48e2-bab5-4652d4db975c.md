## 1. Research Question

What are the origins of European vampire lore, what caused the 18th-century Balkan vampire panics (Serbia, c.1725-1732), and in what distinct senses—documented historical events, misread natural phenomena (decomposition, disease, premature burial), or genuine supernatural occurrence—can vampires be said to have been 'real'?

## 2. Scope

- **Population:** Documented cases of alleged revenants/vampires, exhumations, and corpse 'killings'; the communities and officials involved in panics (villagers, local authorities, military physicians); later folklorists, physicians, and writers who recorded, theorized about, or fictionalized the phenomenon.
- **Geography:** Central and Southeastern Europe, with primary focus on Habsburg-administered Serbia (Balkan frontier) for the 1725-1732 panics; comparative reference to broader Slavic regions, Greece, medieval England, and later New England (for the 19th-century tuberculosis-linked panic) as a cross-regional comparison, not a core geography.
- **Period:** Primary period 1725-1755 (from the Petar Blagojevic and Arnold Paole cases through Maria Theresa's 1755 decree ending state-sanctioned exhumations); secondary background period covering medieval revenant beliefs (e.g., William of Newburgh, 12th century) through early modern Slavic/Greek traditions; downstream period through 1746 (Calmet), 1819 (Polidori), 1892 (Mercy Brown, New England), and 1897 (Stoker) for the transmission and reinterpretation of the lore.

### Out of scope

- Whether vampires exist in a literal supernatural sense as an ontological claim to be adjudicated here; the question addresses explanatory frameworks, not metaphysical verification.
- Later vampire fiction's literary or cultural analysis beyond its role in transmitting and popularizing the 'vampire' concept (e.g., detailed literary criticism of Polidori's The Vampyre or Stoker's Dracula).
- Historical figures sometimes retroactively linked to vampire myth (Vlad III Tepes, Elizabeth Bathory) except insofar as their status as myth sources versus unrelated historical figures is itself part of the question.
- Vampire traditions outside Europe (e.g., Chinese jiangshi, African and Caribbean revenant/zombie traditions) except where directly comparative evidence is cited in mainstream sources on European lore.
- Modern popular culture, film, and contemporary 'real vampire' subculture claims unrelated to the historical panics and their causal explanations.
- Claims in popular or tabloid sources about specific exhumed bodies or panics that cannot be corroborated by primary records or scholarly secondary sources; such claims are to be flagged as unverifiable rather than incorporated as findings.

### Ambiguities the question recorded

- Whether 'vampires were real' is being asked as a question about the reality of the belief/panic phenomenon (uncontroversially yes) or about the reality of an underlying supernatural entity (contested), since the answer differs sharply depending on which reading is adopted.
- Whether the scope of 'origins of European vampire lore' should be traced back to pre-Slavic/pagan revenant beliefs broadly, or bounded to the documented medieval-to-early-modern textual tradition (William of Newburgh onward), as the former implies a much deeper and less verifiable ancestry.
- Whether disease theories (rabies, porphyria, tuberculosis) are to be treated as competing exclusive explanations for the same phenomenon or as explanations for different, historically distinct panics (e.g., porphyria/rabies for Balkan cases vs. tuberculosis for the New England case).

### Assumptions the question recorded

- The 1725-1732 Serbian panics (Blagojevic, Paole cases) are treated as the paradigmatic case study for the 18th-century panics, with Fluckinger's 1732 report treated as the primary documentary source rather than as itself a fully reliable factual account.
- Maria Theresa's 1755 decree and van Swieten's investigation are assumed to mark the administrative/legal endpoint of state involvement in vampire exhumations, bounding the primary period studied.
- The Mercy Brown case (1892, New England) is included as a geographically and temporally distinct comparative case illustrating a disease-based (tuberculosis) explanation, not as part of the core Balkan panic phenomenon.
- Each natural-cause explanation (decomposition, rabies, porphyria, tuberculosis, premature burial) is to be evaluated as a separately testable hypothesis against the specific documented symptoms/behaviors attributed to vampires, rather than assuming one explanation accounts for all cases.

## 3. Definitions

- \'Vampire lore' is treated as a body of beliefs and practices concerning reanimated corpses (revenants) causing harm to the living, including terms such as Slavic upir/upior, Greek vrykolakas, and English 'vampire' as it entered print usage after the 1725-1732 panics.
- \'Vampire panic' refers to a localized episode of community fear involving suspected revenant activity, typically resulting in exhumation and ritual destruction (staking, decapitation, burning) of a corpse, often documented by local or military officials.
- \'Documented events' sense of 'real' refers to verifiable administrative/medical records of exhumations and corpse treatments (e.g., Fluckinger's Visum et Repertum, 1732), independent of whether the underlying supernatural claim was true.
- \'Misread decomposition/disease' sense of 'real' refers to natural post-mortem processes (bloating, skin slippage, purge fluid) or epidemiological patterns (tuberculosis, rabies, porphyria) that could plausibly have been misinterpreted as evidence of vampirism, each treated as a distinct, separately testable causal hypothesis rather than a unified explanation.
- \'Deviant burial' refers to archaeologically identified burials with apotropaic modifications (sickles across the body, stones in the mouth, staking) interpreted by some researchers as evidence of anti-revenant practice, as seen at sites such as Drawsko and Pien in Poland.
