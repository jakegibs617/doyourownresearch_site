## 1. Research Question

What explains the shift in the gender composition of European witch-hunt accusations from predominantly male defendants in early trials (e.g., Valais 1428, Russia) to roughly 80% female defendants during the peak hunts of 1560–1650, and how well do the leading explanations (demonological misogyny such as the Malleus Maleficarum, versus local accusation dynamics involving neighbours, accusers, household conflict, age/widowhood, and the shift from heresy to maleficium prosecutions) account for this change and for the persistence of male-majority regions (Iceland, Normandy, Estonia, Finland, Russia)?

## 2. Scope

- **Population:** Individuals formally accused, tried, or executed for witchcraft (maleficium and/or diabolical heresy) in European jurisdictions, including both secular and ecclesiastical court defendants, accusers, and witnesses named in surviving trial records
- **Geography:** Europe broadly, with specific comparative attention to Valais (Swiss Confederacy), Russia, Ukraine, the Holy Roman Empire (German-speaking lands), France (including Normandy), Scotland, England, Iceland, Estonia, and Finland; claims about regions outside documented trial record sets are out of scope
- **Period:** c. 1400–1700, with early trials (1400s–1520s) distinguished from the peak persecution period (1560–1650) and late/declining prosecutions (1650–1700) treated as a secondary comparison period

### Out of scope

- Witch-hunting or witchcraft belief outside Europe (e.g., colonial North America, Africa, Asia), except as incidental comparison
- Modern (post-1700) revival movements, neopaganism, or contemporary witchcraft accusations
- Folkloric or literary depictions of witches not tied to documented legal/trial evidence
- Detailed theological history of demonology unrelated to gender-share outcomes
- Psychological or anthropological general theories of scapegoating not specific to the European witch-hunt record
- Economic history of early modern Europe except as it bears directly on the Federici thesis and confiscation claims

### Ambiguities the question recorded

- Whether 'drove' the shift should be read as primary causation, necessary condition, or merely correlated/contributing factor — this changes what counts as confirming or disconfirming evidence
- Whether regional trial-record samples (often small, non-random, and unevenly surviving) can support population-level gender-share estimates at all, or only localized claims
- Whether 'popular claims' (nine million, midwife hunt, etc.) should be verdicted against the best current scholarly consensus or against the original claimants' own evidentiary standards
- Whether shifts in recording practice (e.g., which courts kept records, what charges were logged) confound apparent shifts in actual accusation gender ratios

### Assumptions the question recorded

- Surviving trial records, though incomplete and unevenly distributed across regions and time, are treated as the best available proxy for actual accusation patterns
- The distinction between heresy-driven and maleficium-driven prosecution is treated as a meaningful and recoverable variable in the historiography, not merely a retrospective analytic construct
- Claims that cannot be checked against surviving quantitative or documentary evidence (e.g., due to lost records or inherently unverifiable scope, such as the full 'nine million' estimate's original sourcing) will be explicitly marked as unverifiable rather than adjudicated true or false
- Female accusers and household-level conflict are assumed to be a legitimate and recordable category distinct from top-down demonological influence, based on existing social-history scholarship on witch trials

## 3. Definitions

- \'Witch accusation' means a formal denunciation, indictment, or trial record naming a person as a practitioner of harmful magic (maleficium) or diabolical heresy
- \'Gender share' is measured as the proportion of named defendants recorded as female versus male in surviving court or chronicle records for a given trial sample or region
- \'Demonological misogyny' refers to textual arguments in theological/legal treatises (e.g., Malleus Maleficarum, 1487) asserting women's innate susceptibility to the Devil
- \'Local accusation pattern' refers to bottom-up dynamics: neighbour disputes, household harm allegations, age/widowhood status, accuser identity (including female accusers), and community-level social tension preceding formal prosecution
- \'Maleficum-based prosecution' is distinguished from 'heresy-based prosecution' (the latter tied to the Sabbat/pact narrative and diabolism)
- Each popular claim (nine million victims, midwife-hunt thesis, Federici's capitalism/primitive-accumulation thesis, property confiscation/profit motive, Murray's pagan-cult thesis) is treated as a discrete, separately verifiable historical claim requiring a verdict with supporting or refuting numbers where evidence exists

### `H1_demonological_misogyny` — The diffusion of demonological treatises (notably Malleus Maleficarum, 1487, and subsequent demonology literature) that theorized women as inherently susceptible to diabolical pact was the primary driver of the shift to ~80% female defendants during 1560-1650, by shaping elite/clerical prosecutorial theory and judicial training across jurisdictions.

- Falsified by: Regions and periods with documented high circulation, citation, or judicial use of demonological texts (e.g., parts of the Holy Roman Empire, France) but female defendant shares remaining at or below 50%, OR regions with negligible demonological-text penetration (e.g., Iceland, Estonia, Finland, Russia) nonetheless showing the same timing and magnitude of female-share increase as high-penetration regions, would disconfirm the causal link.
- Disconfirming evidence would be observable at: Comparative jurisdiction-level trial record datasets cross-referenced with library/court inventory records of demonological text ownership and citation, as compiled in regional witch-trial historiography (e.g., Behringer, Levack, Rowlands regional studies)
- Required conditions — **all of these must hold**, and the weakest is where
  the argument actually breaks:
  1. Demonological texts were actually read/cited by the specific prosecuting courts in question, not merely published somewhere in Europe
  2. The timing of text circulation precedes (not follows or coincides accidentally with) the documented rise in female defendant share in the same jurisdiction
  3. Male-majority regions show measurably lower demonological-text penetration than female-majority regions
  4. The shift from heresy to maleficium framing, which carries independent gender associations, does not fully account for the correlation attributed to demonology
- Passages declaring a bearing on it: 89.

### `H2_local_accusation_dynamics` — The gender shift is primarily explained by bottom-up local accusation dynamics - neighbour disputes, household conflict, accusations disproportionately targeting older/widowed women with weak kin protection, and female accusers targeting other women - which became dominant as prosecution shifted from heresy/Sabbat framing to maleficium framing after the early period.

- Falsified by: Trial samples showing that local social-conflict variables (widowhood rate, age, accuser identity, household disputes) do NOT differ systematically between early male-majority trials and peak female-majority trials, i.e., the same proportion of accusations stemming from neighbour/household conflict in both periods despite the gender-share shift, would disconfirm this as the operative mechanism.

  1. Surviving trial records contain sufficient detail on accuser identity, relationship to defendant, and stated grievance to code for 'local conflict' type across both periods
