The question / Q
What can the record establish about Jesus, the Bible, the institutions that curated it, and the claims later made in its name?
The short answer / A

Jesus very likely existed, the earliest Christian texts are much earlier than the first complete canon list, and later institutions used scripture in ways the record can document. The existence of God, the authority of commands, and the problem of suffering remain arguments or teachings rather than settled historical findings.

01 / 01

The first clock

Jesus was not written into existence three centuries later.

The historical case for Jesus is stronger than the popular claim that everything about him was invented hundreds of years after his death.

The underlying run finds a very likely historical person, with baptism and crucifixion under Pilate among the few points of broad agreement. Paul's undisputed letters appear roughly 20–30 years after Jesus's death, and Mark roughly 40 years after. Tacitus later records the execution under Pilate.

That does not make every gospel episode historical, and it does not establish the theological claims made about Jesus. It does establish that the date of the first complete canon list is not the date when Christian writing began.

The texts, the collection, and the final list are three different historical events.
02 / 02

The canon

The 300-year claim confuses a list with the writing it lists.

Athanasius's 367 CE letter is the first surviving list of exactly today's 27 New Testament books, but it records a collection already used for generations.

The Hebrew scriptures closed in stages and differed across communities. Christian books circulated, were quoted, disputed, and sorted long before the fourth century. Irenaeus quotes nearly all of today's New Testament; Eusebius distinguishes accepted, disputed, and rejected books; regional councils later ratify lists already in use.

Binding closure comes later and separately: Trent for Catholics in 1546, Protestant confessions across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and the Synod of Jerusalem for Eastern Orthodoxy in 1672. That is why Bibles still differ.

The first exact list is not the first moment of use.
03 / 03

The selectors

The curators had prejudices, but the record rarely names prejudice as the selection rule.

Some selectors held views that are indefensible by modern standards and were contested by their own contemporaries.

Luther's anti-Jewish writing was extreme; Jerome's writing on marriage and women alarmed friends; Augustine defended coercion against heretics. Marcion's canon is known only through an opponent. Those facts matter when assessing institutions and people.

But the runs found no selector's own words saying a book was included or excluded because of that prejudice. The historical record supports a more complicated account: use, inherited authority, rivalry, translation, and later institutional ratification all mattered.

Bad beliefs can shape institutions without leaving a clean causal label in the canon record.
04 / 04

Scripture in use

The violence is historical, but the chain from verse to harm is not a single step.

The overview identifies explicit cases where readings of scripture were used to justify killing, conquest, slavery, or coercion.

The witch trials drew on Exodus 22:18; the Requerimiento supplied a theological-legal frame for conquest; the Curse of Ham entered proslavery arguments; and crusade preaching promised remission of sins for fighting. These are documented uses, not proof that a verse mechanically caused every later act.

The largest tolls were not measured in this run, so no honest ranking is possible. The right conclusion is narrower: interpretation, law, institutions, and political power turned texts into warrants in particular historical settings.

A verse becomes a warrant through a chain of interpretation and power.
05 / 05

Authority

God's existence and God's commands are different questions.

The fifth run separates philosophical arguments for God's existence from the institutional chain that says what God commands.

Catholic teaching claims that God's existence is knowable through natural reason, and the run records arguments from Anselm and Aquinas alongside their objections. Those arguments are not circular in form, but they remain contested arguments rather than historical measurements.

The question of where ‘you should’ comes from is different. Scripture, tradition, and the teaching office certify one another inside a system. That may be coherent as theology; it is not independent historical confirmation.

An argument for a foundation is not the same thing as an institution's authority to interpret it.
06 / 06

The problem of evil

Theodicies answer contradiction more easily than suffering.

Philosophers largely accept that God and evil are not a strict logical contradiction, but that is a smaller claim than explaining the amount and distribution of suffering.

The run finds the strongest defenses weakest where disease, famine, and childhood suffering are concerned—events no individual chooses. Plantinga calls most theodicies ‘tepid, shallow and ultimately frivolous’; the Catechism says no quick answer will suffice.

The overview therefore records an unresolved problem rather than manufacturing a verdict. The question of why the wicked go unpunished also remained unanswered in this run.

A logical possibility is not an explanation of the world as experienced.
07 / 07

The afterlife

The chronology of heaven is a history of doctrine, not a census.

Later Catholic teaching says the righteous dead waited until Christ's ascension, while mainstream theology says souls are newly created rather than pre-existing.

The question arose partly because the early church expected Christ to return soon. Medieval writers debated the geography and capacity of the afterlife, but no church teaching found in this run addressed whether heaven could become crowded. Demographers estimate roughly 117 billion people have ever been born.

Origen's view of pre-existing souls was condemned. The surviving record therefore shows doctrinal development and disagreement; it does not provide a checkable account of where a soul was before birth.

A doctrine can have a history without becoming a historical observation.
